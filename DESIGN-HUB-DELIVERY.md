# Emberfall - art delivery for the hub background

This is the technical contract for handing over the background art for the hub screen: what
frame to paint at, which parts of it will be covered by buttons and readouts, what the file
needs to be, and where it goes. It does not tell you what to draw beyond the parts that follow
directly from the fiction. For the visual rules that apply to characters (colour, silhouette,
material feel), read DESIGN-ART-BIBLE.md; this document works out which of those rules actually
carry over to a painted place rather than a painted person, and which don't, since nobody has
written that down before. DESIGN-ART-DELIVERY.md is this document's sibling: read that one for
how a character sprite gets delivered, this one for how the hub background does. The two follow
the same conventions on purpose.

**This is being written ahead of the thing it describes.** The hub screen does not exist yet.
The game is moving from portrait to landscape, and that move (build order task 9) has not been
built either. Nobody can open the hub in the editor and check a number against it, because there
is no editor build of it to open. Everything below that depends on a real screen existing is
flagged as an estimate, not a measurement, and the last section collects all of those flags in
one place. This document exists so you can start painting now instead of waiting for the build
to catch up to the plan.

## 1. What the hub is

The hub is the home screen: the place you land between battles, where your heroes and your
resources are always on screen. Nothing like it exists in the game today. The one screen that
plays a similar role right now has no painted art behind it at all, just a flat dark colour.
The battle screen has a painted-feeling sky, but that sky is generated by the game itself, a
smooth colour gradient with no image file involved anywhere. There is no background art of any
kind in this game yet, and no prior version of the hub to match or improve on. This is the first
one.

## 2. The frame: landscape, and how wide is genuinely wide

The game is landscape-only once task 9 ships, and the hub is a landscape screen from the start.
Paint horizontally, not vertically.

**A phone doesn't have one landscape shape.** Every Android phone reports its screen a bit
differently once it's turned sideways, and the differences are bigger than they sound.

- An older or smaller phone, once rotated to landscape, is close to 16:9, meaning it's about
  1.78 times wider than it is tall.
- A tall modern phone, the kind with a very long screen in your pocket, is close to 20:9 once
  rotated, meaning it's about 2.2 times wider than it is tall.
- Here is the part worth sitting with, because it's the opposite of what most people guess: **the
  taller a phone is in your pocket, the wider it gets once it's turned sideways for this game.**
  The screen's short edge and long edge just swap which way they're facing. So the phones that
  reveal the *most* extra width in landscape are the newest, tallest ones, not the oldest ones.

That means the extra space you have to account for shows up on the **left and right**, not the
top and bottom. A landscape frame doesn't get taller or shorter across devices in any way that
matters here, it gets wider or narrower.

**What to paint.** Work in a canvas 2400 pixels wide by 1080 pixels tall. Treat the middle 1920
pixels of that width, the same 1080 tall, as your **safe core**: this part of the canvas is
visible on every phone, no matter how it's shaped. That leaves 240 pixels of extra painted width
on the left edge and 240 on the right, your **bleed**. A phone shaped closer to 16:9 will not
show that bleed at all, everything past the safe core simply goes unseen. A phone shaped closer
to 20:9 will show most or all of it. Nothing between those two extremes ever crops *into* the
safe core, only into the bleed around it, so as long as the safe core carries the composition,
you can't lose anything that matters.

Practically: don't put anything you care about right at the far left or right edge of the full
2400-wide canvas, since that's the part most likely to go unseen. Don't leave the outer 240
pixels on each side completely blank either, since on a wide phone that space is genuinely on
screen. A loose extension of whatever's happening in the safe core, rather than a hard cut or a
new idea, is the right way to treat it.

## 3. Safe zones: where a button will sit on top of your paint

The hub carries a resource bar across the top, rows of buttons down the left and right edges,
and a row of buttons along the bottom. Every one of those sits **on top of** the background, not
next to it, so whatever you paint underneath a button is never seen, not faintly, not at reduced
opacity. Detail spent there is time spent on something nobody will ever see.

**These numbers are a working estimate**, sized against the proportions the rest of the game's
interface already uses, not a measurement of the hub itself, because the hub doesn't exist yet
to measure. Treat them as where to start, not as a locked contract; the note at the end of this
document says so again so it isn't missed.

Working from that estimate, inside your 1920-wide safe core:

- **Top band, full width, the top 14%** (roughly the top 150 pixels of the 1080-tall frame).
  This is the resource bar. It runs the entire width of the screen, so nothing along the very top
  edge, anywhere, is worth detailing.
- **Bottom band, full width, the bottom 15%** (roughly the bottom 160 pixels). The bottom row of
  action buttons.
- **Left rail, the left 15%** (roughly the left 290 pixels). Buttons stacked down this edge.
- **Right rail, the right 15%** (roughly the right 290 pixels). The same, mirrored.

That leaves an open middle area, roughly the centre 70% of the width and 70% of the height of
the safe core (about 1340 by 765 pixels), that is never covered by anything. This is where your
composition actually gets seen and where detail earns its time. Think of it the way a poster
designer thinks about the area a title and a logo are going to sit on top of: paint through those
regions so the scene reads as continuous, but don't spend your best work where it's guaranteed
to be hidden.

**The four corners are covered twice over**, where the top or bottom band overlaps a side rail.
A rough 290-by-150-pixel box in each corner of the safe core is the least-seen real estate on the
whole canvas. Broad colour and value there, so nothing looks obviously unfinished if a sliver
ever peeks out from behind a rounded button corner, is enough. Don't paint a focal point into a
corner.

None of this applies to the bleed strips described in section 2. Nothing UI-related is ever
drawn out there; it's pure background, full stop.

## 4. The file

- **PNG, 8-bit colour.** Same file type the character art already uses.
- **No transparency needed.** A character sprite has to be transparent because it's placed on
  top of a 3D scene behind it. The hub background is the opposite: it's the very back layer, with
  nothing behind it to show through. Deliver it as a fully opaque image.
- **Standard colour, nothing special.** Paint and export the way you normally would for any
  on-screen art; there's no unusual colour profile requirement here.
- **Deliver at the frame size**, 2400 by 1080 pixels, matching section 2. Unlike the character
  pipeline, there is currently no established size cap or compression setting for background art,
  since nothing has needed one before this. That's a genuinely open question, noted again at the
  end of this document, not a gap you need to solve yourself.
- **Where it goes and what it's called** follows the exact same pattern the character art
  already uses, just in its own folder rather than invented from scratch: character art lives in
  a folder called `Sprites/Units`, named by the character's own internal id in lowercase with
  underscores. The hub background should follow the same shape, in a sibling folder,
  `Sprites/Backgrounds`, named for the screen it belongs to: `hub.png`. Ask the engineer to
  confirm this is where it's actually wired up to load from before you treat it as final, since
  the loading code for this doesn't exist yet either, this is the naming and folder pattern to
  aim for, carried over on purpose rather than invented fresh.

**One more thing worth knowing about how this sits once it's in the game.** The interface itself
is painted in a very dark, near-black, slightly purple palette, close to hex `0B0813` for the
screen behind everything and hex `191325` for the panels that float on top of it, with warm
off-white text close to hex `F3EEE6`. Your background is going to sit directly alongside that
palette, in the open middle area described in section 3, and directly behind it, in the areas the
panels and buttons cover. A background painted very bright or very pale overall will fight that
dark interface for attention and wash out the light text sitting on top of it; strong, saturated
light sources and highlights within the scene are good and will read well against a dark
interface, but the overall key of the piece should stay compatible with sitting next to a
near-black panel rather than against it.

## 5. Flat now, layered art later

**The hub background is one flat image**, full stop, at least for the first version of this
screen. Whatever is actually loaded and shown to the player is a single PNG with everything
painted into it: sky, midground, foreground, all one file.

**Parallax, several layers moving at different speeds as something scrolls or the screen tilts,
is a real possibility for later**, not this delivery. Nothing is being asked of you for it now.
The only thing worth doing on your end, cheaply, is keeping your own working file separated into
the layers you'd naturally use anyway (a sky pass, a midground, a foreground), rather than
flattening and merging everything as you go. If parallax gets picked up as a real ask later,
having those layers still sitting in your source file means redelivering existing art broken
into pieces instead of repainting it from scratch. That's the only thing to plan for; the
delivery itself stays one flat file until you hear otherwise.

## 6. Reconciling with the art bible

DESIGN-ART-BIBLE.md was written entirely about characters, one hero at a time, and says so in
its own opening line. Most of it doesn't transfer to a painted place, and applying it by default
would be a mistake, not a safe default. Going through it rule by rule:

**Proportions, cel shading, the crisp dark outline, the three-value-group silhouette limit.**
These describe how a single character model is built and shaded so it reads at a glance. None of
it applies to a painted environment, which isn't a silhouette against a background, it *is* the
background. Paint it with the value range and material realism a background needs; don't flatten
it toward a character's shading treatment.

**The six silhouette rules** (Ember's irreversible asymmetry, Tide's directional contradiction,
and so on). These are explicitly about a character's outline reading correctly at thumbnail size
with the colour and effects turned off. A background has no single outline to read this way.
Doesn't apply.

**The three signatures** (the Cinder Seam, the Memory Error, the Cinder Notch). All three are
marks on a *person's body or gear*, evidence of how the Cinder reconstructed that specific
individual. A place was never reconstructed by the Cinder the way a summoned hero was; the
fiction doesn't support putting a reconstruction seam into a rock formation. Doesn't apply, for a
sturdier reason than the others: it isn't just the wrong scale, it's the wrong kind of thing
entirely.

**The two rules worth thinking about hardest, because they're not simple yes-or-no calls:**

**"No elemental cosplay."** Read literally, this bans an Ember character from wearing literal
flames because they're Ember. That's a rule about a *character's costume specifically*, stated
that way in the bible itself. It is not a rule against an environment looking like its aspect,
and DESIGN-WORLD actually establishes the opposite for regions: each of the game's six named
regions genuinely *is* what its aspect wants, made real. Ash and fire in a place ruled by Ember
belief isn't costume shorthand there, it's the setting being honest about what it is. So this ban
does not block the hub from reading as ash-and-ember, if the hub turns out to sit in that region.
Whether it does is a separate, still-open question, covered in section 7.

**The accent-coverage rule (roughly 10 to 15% of a hero, never tint the whole character).** This
number was sized for a single figure, where "10 to 15% of one person's silhouette" is a
meaningful, checkable amount. It was never written with a full-screen environment painting in
mind, and applying that literal percentage to a landscape composition doesn't carry any real
meaning, a painting has far more surface for light, atmosphere and material colour to legitimately
occupy than a person does. What's worth keeping is the *principle* underneath the number: an
aspect's colour should read as a considered accent within the scene, not as a wash that tints the
whole image one colour and drowns everything else the material world in that aspect's table is
actually about (forged steel, mirrored glass, patched hide, whichever applies). Keep the
principle. Don't try to hit the percentage.

## 7. Where the hub actually is

DESIGN-WORLD names six regions, one per chapter, each an honest expression of one aspect's
belief. Chapter 1, currently playing, is the Ash Marches, Ember's region. It would be a
reasonable first guess that the hub sits there too, painted ash-and-ember to match.

**This isn't settled, and there's a real reason to hesitate before assuming it.** The hub isn't
part of any one chapter. It's the screen you return to between every battle in every chapter, the
whole way through the campaign, including the five chapters that haven't been built yet and are
set in five other regions with five other material worlds. A hub painted specifically as the Ash
Marches reads correctly for as long as the player is in chapter 1, and reads like a mistake
the moment they reach the Wandering Coast in chapter 2 and are still looking at ash and ember
every time they check their roster.

The fiction actually has a cleaner answer available, though it isn't written down anywhere as a
decision yet, only as raw material to draw one from. The player is the Keeper of the Cinder, an
office, not tied to any one place, and the Cinder itself is described as having taken no side
among the six aspects, no colour of its own when at rest. That points toward the hub being closer
to neutral ground, the Keeper's own vantage rather than a stop in any single region, staying
visually consistent no matter which chapter the player is actually fighting through.

**Stated plainly: this has not been decided.** The reasoning above is this document's best read
of the existing fiction, not a confirmed answer, and it deserves an actual decision from whoever
owns the story before paint goes down, not an assumption baked in by the first person who had to
guess. If you need to start before that answer exists, the neutral reading is the safer one to
sketch toward, since a specifically Ember-coded hub is the one outcome that's guaranteed to need
rework the moment the story leaves chapter 1.

## 8. Not yet decided, and what couldn't be determined here

Collected in one place, so nothing above is mistaken for more settled than it is.

- **Whether the hub sits in a specific region or reads as neutral ground.** Section 7. A reasoned
  recommendation, not a decision.
- **The exact safe-zone proportions in section 3.** Estimated from the size of the resource bar,
  the button rows and the padding the rest of the game's interface already uses, scaled to a
  landscape frame that doesn't exist yet. Once the hub is actually built, these numbers should be
  checked against the real thing and corrected here if they're off.
- **Whether the resource bar, side rails and bottom row end up exactly where this document
  assumes.** They're described here because you asked what to expect and named all three
  yourself; the actual layout is still task 9 and the hub screen's own build, neither of which
  exist yet.
- **Any file size cap or compression setting for background art**, the way character art already
  has a settled cap. Nobody has needed one before this, so nothing has been decided.
  Deliver at the frame size in section 2 and expect this to be revisited once an engineer wires
  up the loading for it.
- **The exact folder path this file loads from.** `Sprites/Backgrounds/hub.png` follows the
  established pattern and is the right name to aim for; the loading code that would confirm it
  doesn't exist yet.
- **Whether parallax actually gets picked up**, and if so, how many layers and which elements
  split into which one. Section 5 only asks you to keep your options open, not to build for a
  request that hasn't been made.
- **How this reads once landscape (task 9) actually ships and someone can look at it on a real
  or simulated phone.** Every number in this document is reasoned from what exists today, not
  seen in place, because there is nothing to look at yet.
